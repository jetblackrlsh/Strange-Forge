# Strange Heroes Lore Guide

## Playtest canon and a Strangeforge campaign reference

An unofficial, original-language reference to Tate RF’s **Strange Heroes** playtest and its official free supplements. Contains adventure spoilers. Source edition: playtest v1.02; guide prepared 27 October 2026. Page numbers below are PDF pages, including covers.

## 1. How much lore is there?

Strange Heroes currently offers **a flexible superhero framework with a small but useful cast and several setting hooks**, rather than an extensively documented shared universe. The 94-page playtest is chiefly a rules manual. Its NPC dossier occupies pp.78–92, but most of those pages are statistics and abilities, with short biographical notes rather than long histories. Some sheets repeat; this should not be mistaken for fifteen pages of distinct lore.

The three-page chapter called “History” (pp.31–33) concerns a character’s origins and training, not the history of Earth. The final page advertises information about **Strange Earth** as content intended for the eventual final book. That announcement is not a setting chapter already present in the playtest. Likewise, the promised larger villain roster should not be counted as published playtest lore.

The separate four-character pregen booklet and two adventures add considerably more practical context: named protagonists, New York contacts and locations, antagonistic organizations, and situations that reveal how the world works. Together, these provide enough material for a campaign starting point, while leaving major questions to the table.

## 2. What the setting actually establishes

### Earth with extraordinary possibilities

The published adventures use New York City, including Central Park, the Bronx and Midtown Manhattan. Ordinary police, intelligence agencies, celebrities, scientists and zookeepers exist alongside superheroes, mutants, gods, aliens and futuristic technology. These are the sources’ fictional versions of real places and institutions—not factual claims about the real institutions.

Origins are deliberately plural. The character-creation chapter permits extraterrestrial ancestry, underwater adaptations, advanced technology, magic, legendary monsters, divine ancestry, exceptional training, scientific accidents, genetic differences, and bonds with unusual objects or creatures. These are available character concepts, not a fixed historical sequence explaining every power. A passing suggestion of Atlantean ancestry, for example, is not a developed account of an Atlantean civilization. Players and GMs are expressly allowed to invent suitable origins and training. [Core, pp.30–33]

### Nexus, magic and other planes

**Nexus** represents a hero’s relationship to luck, fate and the universe. Its concrete role is an attribute used for luck-dependent attempts and linked to frustration dice. It does not establish a mapped multiverse, pantheon, cosmic council or universal origin of superpowers. [Core, p.29]

Astral projection and other supernatural abilities are supported by the rules. The Thor pregen also names Odin, the Aesir, Midgard and giants. These establish particular supernatural possibilities, not a comprehensive cosmological system binding every campaign. [Core, Telepathy talents; Pregens, pp.1–2]

### Public opinion matters

The adventures treat civilian rescue, public trust and relationships as meaningful. New York can celebrate heroes, organizations can oppose them, and enemies can exploit fear of superpowered people. Concert Killer adds a reputation system with separate standings among groups. Its catastrophic endings are possible outcomes of that adventure, not events already fixed in world history. [Zoo, pp.2–7; Concert, pp.12–13]

## 3. The four official playable pregenerated heroes

These are the heroes in the separate **Pregenerated Characters.pdf**, not a roster inferred from villain sheets. Appearance descriptions distinguish printed biographical facts from the artwork shown in that booklet. Their visual depictions are not descriptions of similarly named characters from unrelated franchises.

### Thor

**Who he is:** Odin’s son and a prince of the Aesir. He has come to Midgard/Earth to find his enchanted belt, stolen by giants; the belt is associated with his divine strength. He carries a magical hammer and has lightning-related abilities, flight and exceptional strength. [Pregens, pp.1–2]

**Appearance:** The sheet identifies blond hair and blue eyes. Its illustration depicts a bare-chested, muscular figure with green clothing and cape, tall boots, a dark helmet and a hammer. This is the booklet’s mythological/comic portrayal, not an instruction to reproduce a different franchise’s costume.

### Micro-Man — Ted Wood

**Who he is:** A district attorney who rejected a criminal bribe. After gangsters killed his scientist brother, Ted adopted his brother’s size-changing gas and technology to pursue criminals. His mask and equipment support enhanced perception; his toolkit includes changing size. [Pregens, pp.3–4]

**Appearance:** Brown hair and eyes; a technologically equipped mask. The accompanying art shows a green costume, purplish hood/mask, conspicuous blue eye lenses, red gloves and boots, and dark waist detailing.

### Captain Midnight — Captain Alex Archer

**Who he is:** A U.S. Air Force pilot transformed by cosmic energy during an experimental-aircraft test. He can generate radiation, and the sheet places him in a prominent military superhero role. His abilities include energy control and teleportation. [Pregens, pp.5–6]

**Appearance:** The biography specifies brown eyes, gray hair and a modified flight suit. The art depicts a red suit, dark hood/headgear, gloves and boots, and a winged chest emblem with a pale circular center. Do not silently replace this identity or origin with that of another publication’s Captain Midnight.

### Red Orchid — Josefina Santiago

**Who she is:** A naturally gifted telepath who became a pilot to escape the overwhelming mental noise of other people. Preventing a bombing demonstrated the constructive potential of her powers and led her into vigilantism. Her toolkit includes telepathy, astral projection and flight. [Pregens, pp.7–8]

**Appearance:** Black hair, brown eyes and an orchid at the lapel. The illustration shows a black eye mask, red high-collared top with full upper sleeves, gloves and loose red trousers.

**Important distinction:** No published connection is established between **Red Orchid the hero** and **Orchid the anti-superhuman organization**. Similar names alone are not evidence of affiliation.

## 4. Other named figures in the core dossier

### Heroic figures and morally complicated vigilantes

**Funnel-Web — Richard Ryan:** Taken from his Aboriginal parents by the government as an infant, Richard grew up alienated. After helping a spider spirit, he acquired spiderlike powers. His stated purpose includes opposing the government and reuniting separated families. The sheet identifies red cloth wrappings; its portrait is redacted. This individual background should not be expanded into invented claims about real Indigenous spiritual traditions. [Core, p.81]

**Microface — Thomas Oak:** A technologically equipped vigilante whose DEA-agent brother James was murdered by a crime boss. Thomas repurposed his brother’s equipment into a special mask and pursued revenge. His mask includes a microphone; his dossier supports enhanced senses, martial arts and pistol use. He is **not Micro-Man/Ted Wood**. [Core, p.88]

**The Spider — Warren Wentworth:** A wealthy investor who responded to violent crime by becoming a criminal kingpin himself. He is a morally compromised figure rather than an uncomplicated exemplar of heroism. His sheet describes a black cape with a red web design and twin pistols. He is **not Funnel-Web**. [Core, p.92]

### Villains

**Black Nimbus:** A black-smoke-bodied alien whose ship crashed on Earth. She commits bank robberies to fund its reconstruction. Her motive supplies an immediate antagonist hook, but the dossier does not name her homeworld or provide its history. [Core, p.79; repeated sheets pp.87, 89]

**Dr Dreadful — Dr Francois Murci:** A geneticist who has prolonged his life for more than a century through cloning. His project is a genetically engineered master race, and he seeks an ideal host for it. His distinctive features include sharklike teeth; abilities include mental influence and teleportation. [Core, p.80]

**Gold Star — Aurelia Orellana:** A visitor from twenty thousand years in the future who wants to become humanity’s most celebrated person by humiliating established heroes. Her robotic companion **Lenny** supplies much of her technology. This is a short time-travel premise, not a detailed canon timeline of the next twenty millennia. [Core, p.82]

**Magnos — Minato Abe:** A magnetic-powered man who grew up in Japan during World War II. His powers protected him during the bombing that killed his family. He now seeks humanity’s replacement, considering it destructive, and is associated with the Scarred Brotherhood. The sheet mentions radiation burns on his left arm. [Core, p.83]

The dossier also includes generic ninja assassins and reusable faction-member profiles. These provide opponents, not additional fully developed cultures or countries. Many main-book portraits are marked REDACTED even though their names, biographies and rules remain readable. Missing portrait art is not permission to claim an invented costume is official. [Core, pp.84–91]

## 5. Organizations and their relationships

### Manticore

An international fascist/neo-Nazi terrorist organization intent on displacing the world’s governments. It fields armed agents, officers and mechanized battle suits. Its leadership treats ordinary agents as expendable. The Zoo Rescue depicts a New York operation under Deathstalker; Concert Killer adds a Manticore infiltrator within the fictional NYPD. No complete global hierarchy, headquarters gazetteer or founding chronology is provided. [Core, pp.84–86; Zoo, p.2; Concert, pp.2–4]

### Scarred Brotherhood

A group recruiting people whose genetic differences have left them excluded or persecuted. Its generic member profile describes training and preparations to replace humanity. Magnos is affiliated with it. The available sheets establish a villainous militant direction but do not give a comprehensive membership list, territorial base or political history. **Not every genetically unusual character is therefore a member or villain.** [Core, pp.83, 91]

### Orchid

A coalition of human scientists who fear superpowered beings and aim to eradicate them. It recruits both through fear and access to generous research funding; some recruits receive laboratories, while others become agents. The appendix depicts agents with pink protective suits and photon-ray weapons. Bombshell works with Orchid in Concert Killer. [Concert, pp.2, 16]

Manticore and Orchid appear in the same adventure, but that does not establish that they are one organization, share a leader, or have a permanent alliance. [Concert, pp.2–5]

### Mutant Alliance

Gregory Beak introduces himself as its head and acts as a New York contact for superheroes. The adventure supplies no extensive charter, headquarters or organizational history. Do not confuse this group with the Scarred Brotherhood. [Concert, p.3]

## 6. The Zoo Rescue — local setting and cast

**Scope:** An introductory adventure for three to five tier-1 or tier-2 heroes, centered on the Central Park Zoo and Belvedere Castle in New York City. [Zoo, p.2]

**Premise:** Manticore wants a chemical from endangered golden lemurs for a nerve-agent project. Its operatives deliberately flood the zoo to create cover for a theft. The heroes first protect visitors and animals, then pursue the kidnappers across Central Park to a confrontation at the castle. The biological premise is adventure fiction, not a real-world zoological assertion. [Zoo, pp.2–7]

**Brian Byrne:** The zoo’s head keeper, an Irish-accented man who welcomes the heroes. Manticore captures him because of his knowledge of the lemurs’ care. **Romeo and Juliet** are the breeding pair of golden lemurs. Other named zoo animals include **Skipper** the penguin; bears **Treena, Luna and Amber**; and snow monkeys **Zeppy, Taro, Koshi, Momo and Moji**. [Zoo, pp.2–4]

**Deathstalker — Harris Gorgon:** Manticore’s chief troop officer in New York, elevated after talking his way out of a disastrous capture. He leads this mission using a **Scorpobot**. His sheet identifies a skull mask. The adventure does not establish a connection to similarly named characters elsewhere. [Zoo, pp.5–6, 9]

**Continuing relationship:** Successful rescue can make the heroes popular locally and give them a continuing contact with the zoo. Who escapes and what happens next depend on play; the scenario does not dictate a permanent canonical outcome. [Zoo, p.7]

## 7. Concert Killer — local setting and cast

**Scope:** An investigation and rescue story moving from the fictional NYPD 43rd Precinct in the Bronx to an apartment in Midtown and Madison Square Garden. It expands the social conflict surrounding superheroes. [Concert, pp.2–12]

**Premise:** Orchid hires Bombshell to murder a prominent singer and frame a superhero. The heroes investigate a prisoner, expose or survive a separate threat inside the precinct, discover the assassin’s operation and race to prevent a public catastrophe at the concert. Outcomes are branching possibilities, not fixed history. [Concert, pp.2–12]

**Gregory “Greg” Beak:** A pigeon mutated by toxic chemicals, able to create shadowy duplicates. He has spent approximately a decade supporting New York superheroes and heads the Mutant Alliance. He wears an emerald suit and tie and speaks with a Brooklyn accent. He recruits and supports the protagonists. [Concert, pp.2–3]

**Bombshell — Niki Noon:** A gifted chemist and roboticist expelled from academia after a rejected teacher fabricated plagiarism evidence. She became an assassin and uses advanced chemistry and robots. Her appendix profile also gives the name **Onika Noo-Nanj**, describes chemical equipment along her arms, and identifies her battle robots as her creations. Her portrait is redacted; no complete costume should be presented as canonical. [Concert, pp.2, 14–15]

**Richard-Quinn Larenzo:** An ex-CIA operative now working for Orchid. He is the prisoner who can reveal the assassin’s lead. The scenario emphasizes pride and his willingness to trade information. His background includes a previous undercover assignment at New York University. [Concert, pp.2–5]

**Ian Ciraulo:** A Manticore agent embedded in the police. He has independently stolen explosive evidence and becomes a threat during the interrogation. His interference does not make him the architect of Bombshell’s assignment. [Concert, pp.2, 4–5]

**Billy Page:** The celebrity singer targeted at her Madison Square Garden performance. The introduction calls her a jazz singer, while other passages use pop-star terminology; there is no need to invent a career history to reconcile that wording. [Concert, pp.2, 7–8]

**Abe Harrison:** The venue manager, trapped in a storeroom during the attack and in urgent need of his heart medication. His rescue is one of the scenario’s civilian priorities. [Concert, pp.10–11]

**Other setting details:** The adventure introduces the security-equipment brand Arm-Alarms and uses venue workers, hostages and battle robots to populate its climax. These are local scenario details, not a full technology catalog. [Concert, pp.10–11, 15]

**Editorial caution:** The introductory tier line retains the title “Star Slayer,” while a later combat note discusses tier-2 heroes despite the introductory tier-1 designation. These inconsistencies should be treated as playtest editing issues, not as evidence of a hidden additional adventure or elaborate canon. [Concert, pp.2, 12]

## 8. Creator commentary beyond the playtest

The [10 July 2024 creator Q&A](https://gmshoe.wordpress.com/2024/07/10/qa-tate-renforth-strange-heroes/) explains the pulp/early-comics aesthetic while explicitly leaving the period open. Its examples of the planned Strange Earth include psychic dinosaurs and a fascist invasion of Mars. It also describes **Noir**, a detective bound to a magical spirit who can assume a skeletal, reaperlike form. These are creator-announced ideas, not fully developed entries in the downloadable v1.02 setting. Treat the distinction visibly when adding them to a campaign.

## 9. What remains open for Strangeforge

Strangeforge can support either a campaign using these published elements or an original setting using the same broad superhero possibilities. Keep three layers separate:

- **Published playtest facts:** Named people, stated affiliations and backgrounds, and the actual adventure premises summarized above.
- **Campaign outcomes:** What the players accomplish, whom they save, and which enemies escape. Record these as that table’s continuity.
- **Original Strangeforge material:** New cities, organizations, histories, cosmology, costumes where none are supplied, and connections between unrelated characters. Label these as original additions, not recovered official lore.

Questions the sources leave open include the global history of superpowers, the first superhero, the legal status of powered people worldwide, the origin and structure of the Mutant Alliance, and a detailed map of alien civilizations or supernatural realms. Strangeforge should let the GM answer such questions instead of supplying invented answers under the author’s name.

**Bottom line:** There is enough published lore to give a starting campaign recognizable people, enemies and places. There is not enough to require an encyclopedia before play. That light canon is a strength for building an original campaign world.
